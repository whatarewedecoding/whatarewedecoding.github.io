Abstract

Non-invasive neural language decoding has advanced rapidly, with end-to-end systems now turning MEG and EEG into fluent sentences. However, a high score may come from the decoder's language prior, the text metric, or the input structure rather than the brain, and the output alone cannot distinguish them. In this work, we ask where such scores come from. We find that in an end-to-end sentence decoding pipeline, input duration alone supports most of the score: scored before the decoder, Gaussian noise of the same duration keeps 66.3% sentence-retrieval R@1 against 90.8% for real MEG, a shortcut absent from an earlier invasive ECoG control and missed by noise controls scored at the output. We propose source attribution, which reads the score out at the decoder's input, where evaluation becomes retrieval with a known chance level, and gives each of three sources—structure in the input, evidence within a window, and context across windows—its own control. With duration removed, word-locked MEG windows still identify their audio among 1,464 candidates for 44% of windows, while noise stays at chance. We further state that a contextual gain can be credited to the brain only if it falls with this local evidence and vanishes without it. A training-free aggregation meets this criterion on two MEG datasets, raising R@1 by 7.7 and 6.9 points, and yields no spurious gain on EEG, where local evidence is very weak; a stage that uses sentence length in place of evidence fails it. Crucially, source attribution requires no change to a pipeline's task, architecture, or training, suggesting broad applicability across encoder–decoder pipelines. By turning whether a decoder reads the brain from an assumption into a measurement, it lays a foundation for trustworthy non-invasive language decoding.

Source attribution

Encoder–decoder pipelines are scored on the output text, after the decoder's language prior, its decoding strategy, and the text metric have all entered. We move the readout to the decoder input.

At that point no text has been generated, but the test stimuli form a finite, known set, so the readout ranks the presented stimulus among them. Its chance level is known, and retrieval-based and generation-based decoding meet here: the former stops, the latter continues, so any generation pipeline can be read out the same way. Each source of the score then gets a control that removes that source and keeps everything else.

Structure in the input

A signal-blind control replaces only the neural content and keeps length, padding, and masks. Any score it keeps comes from the input's structure, not the brain.

Evidence within a window

Fixed-duration, word-locked windows and stimulus-identity splits keep structure out, so what remains of the ranking rests on neural content.

Context across windows

Context re-enters as a separate stage on frozen local scores. Its gain is credited to the brain only if it falls with local evidence and vanishes without it.

A length shortcut hidden at the output

We reproduce a Whisper-based end-to-end sentence decoder and score it before its text decoder, with participants held out. Real MEG reaches 90.8% R@1; Gaussian noise within the valid length, with padding and masks unchanged, still reaches 66.3%. The noise keeps only the input's duration, so duration alone supports most of the score. With fixed-duration inputs, noise no longer scores high.

Earlier checks pointed the other way. In invasive ECoG decoding, a network trained on noise of the correct length stayed near chance, and non-invasive noise tests that replace the whole input and score the output text report BLEU-1 near zero. The shortcut becomes visible only when the score is read before the decoder and the control replaces only the neural content.

What the brain signal supports on its own

With fixed-duration windows and stimulus-identity splits, above-chance ranking requires neural content. Gaussian inputs stay at chance, whereas real MEG ranks its time-aligned audio first for 44.4% of windows among 1,464 candidates on Gwilliams and 21.7% among 825 on MOUS. Every participant is above chance.

The local score resolves each window's stimulus content, not the sentence it belongs to: when it is wrong, its top choice falls in the target's sentence 1.0% of the time, close to the 1.1% expected from sentence lengths alone. Ranking within the correct sentence, with logits fixed, raises R@1 from 44% to 88%. That gap is the room a sentence-level context stage can recover.

When can a contextual gain be credited to the brain?

A training-free sentence-bucket aggregation pools a sentence's window scores and shifts whole candidate sentences by additive shallow fusion. On the same frozen logits it raises R@1 from 44.4% to 52.1% on Gwilliams and from 21.7% to 28.6% on MOUS, positive for all three encoders and, with Dense-TCNN, for every participant. The controls then test what that gain rests on.

The gain falls with local evidence and vanishes without it

Breaking the sentence grouping lowers it. Mixing query windows with stimulus-mismatched MEG shrinks it from +7.7 points to zero as local R@1 approaches chance, across four attenuation families. Across participants, the gain rises with local R@1 (Spearman ρ = 0.88 on Gwilliams, 0.90 on MOUS). On Gaussian inputs and on EEG, where local evidence is near chance, it yields nothing.

A stage that reads sentence length fails the same test

Because query groups and candidate sentences share boundaries, sentence length alone points to the right sentence. A stage that selects sentences this way peaks at a 16.8-point gain under attenuation and keeps about 1.5% R@1, some 22× chance, with no evidence at all. Both stages use structure; only aggregation's gain needs the recording.

Using source attribution

The checks require no change to a pipeline's task, architecture, or training, and can be placed at the decoder input of any encoder–decoder pipeline:

  1. Read the score out before the decoder, as retrieval over the test stimuli with a known chance level.
  2. Replace only the neural content, keeping length, padding, and masks, to expose what the input's structure carries.
  3. Credit a contextual gain to the brain only if it falls with the local evidence and vanishes without it.
